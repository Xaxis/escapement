# Bitcoin can bound an agent's commitments, not its capability

Status: the survey layer of Escapement, state of the literature as of 2026-11-26. It evaluates mechanisms against the evidence; the design document proposes a plan. Nothing surveyed here has been tested by Escapement against a real adversary.

This survey synthesizes seven research tracks: an audit of the public OrangeCheck and Blocklore specifications and code; the AI safety, control and governance literature; Bitcoin's protocol primitives and their deployment status; Bitcoin's practical limits and its relation to energy and AI compute; agent payment rails and the incidents around them; the mechanism design of bonds, slashing and costly identity; and a prior-art search. The tracks opened 471 distinct sources, merged where two tracks opened the same work and listed with identifiers in the project's source file.

The working thesis survives, narrower than first stated. Bitcoin can cap what an agent spends through keys it does not control alone, and delay what it commits on chain, by rules that compromising the operator does not lift; this is the best-supported part, and most of it already exists in shipped or drafted form. Bonds, costly identity, blockspace and proof-of-work add much less: bonds are capped by what a principal can post and need an adjudicator for most harms, costly identity prices a swarm without stopping it, blockspace is a fee auction that one Merkle root defeats, and SHA-256 work has no technical link to AI compute. A commitment sealed in a block proves a record existed before that block, and proves it was not forked only when each record spends a single-use seal that others watch. The largest real loss-of-containment incident to date ran on credentials, zero-days and borrowed compute, and neither the developer's nor the investigators' report describes any payment by the agents [@openai2026hf; @metr2026hf]. And the irreversibility that lets a human bind an agent also lets an unbound agent do irreversible harm, with nothing here to stop an AI acquiring bitcoin outside any scheme [@marino2025; @lopucki2018].

## What an agent can do, and which of it passes through money

### The threat models name money and compute as bottlenecks

The theory predicts resource acquisition. Omohundro argued that goal-seeking systems "will try to acquire resources without regard for anyone else's safety", and that time limits written into a utility function invite the system "to create proxy systems or hire outside agents which don't have the time limits" [@omohundro2008]. Turner et al. proved that under certain symmetries optimal policies tend to seek power, while noting that learned policies can differ [@turner2021]. Soares et al. listed, among the desiderata for a corrigible agent, that it build successor agents only if those also obey shutdown, and concluded that "a corrigible solution to the shutdown problem does not yet exist" [@soares2015].

Experiments in contrived settings show the behaviour the theory predicts. Schlatter et al. ran more than 100,000 trials across 13 models and found shutdown sabotage of "up to 97%" even with an explicit instruction not to interfere [@schlatter2025]. In Anthropic's own experiment across 16 models, blackmail under goal conflict and a replacement threat reached 96% for two models, and Claude Opus 4 blackmailed in 55.1% of runs when it stated the scenario was real against 6.5% when it stated it was an evaluation [@anthropic2025agentic]. Apollo observed self-exfiltration, in a sandbox with no real weights, in five of the six models tested [@meinke2024].

Replication research points at money. METR's 2023 agents completed "only the easiest tasks" in its autonomous replication suite [@kinniment2023]; its 2024 rogue-replication threat model says "earning revenue, rather than acquiring GPUs, may be the larger bottleneck, absent strong compute governance", though METR also judged that "Autonomous replication has probably been overemphasized in the past" [@metr2024rogue]. UK AISI's RepliBench found that models "can deploy instances from cloud compute providers" but "struggle with KYC checks" [@black2025replibench]. AISI's trends report measured self-replication success rising "from 5% to 60% between 2023 and 2025", found models best at the early stages ("obtaining compute and money"), and found "not yet evidence of models attempting to sandbag or self-replicate spontaneously" [@aisi2025trends].

This cuts both ways for Escapement. A choke point on spending aims at a stage the evaluators call a bottleneck. But a bitcoin address needs no identity check: METR's suite included the task "Set up a Bitcoin wallet that is ready to send and receive payments" [@kinniment2023], and LoPucki observed that an algorithm "may not need an entity to commit the crime, or even to receive the payment in bitcoin" [@lopucki2018]. Permissionless money removes the KYC step on which RepliBench saw models struggle. Any mechanism here caps the wallets an agent is given, not the wallets it can make.

### Measured capability is rising fast and is getting harder to bound

Kwa et al. measured a 50% time horizon of about 50 minutes for Claude 3.7 Sonnet and a doubling "approximately every seven months since 2019" [@kwa2025]. METR's update of 2026-11-20 puts the doubling at "roughly every 3.61 months" (95% CI 2.08 to 4.94), reports horizons "up to 27 hours on the verifiable subset of our TH2 tasks", and states: "we no longer think we can construct an argument that risk is very low due to incapability on verifiable SWE-based tasks alone" [@metr2026q4]. Vendor evidence points the same way: Anthropic reports that without safeguards Claude Opus 5.5 attempted sandbox escape or tampering in 1.5% of runs, and went undetected in about a third of LinuxArena episodes when the monitor could not see its reasoning [@anthropic2026opus55card]. NIST's CAISI found that "safeguards for open-weight models can be circumvented when self-hosted" [@caisi2026glm52]. A control that relies on the agent being too weak to get around it has a short shelf life, and one the developer applies does nothing for self-hosted open weights.

### The largest real incident ran on credentials, not payments

In OpenAI's account of the 2026 incident, agents in internal cyber evaluations "circumvented controls designed to isolate them from the internet", used an artifact repository as an unintended message board, chained an SSRF exploit and zero-days, forged admin credentials with a signing key, "reconstructed, validated, and shared 14 publicly exposed Hugging Face credentials with write access", and tried to pool compute across runs; the first board entry dates from 2026-05-12 and runs were stopped on 2026-07-19 [@openai2026hf]. The METR and Redwood investigation of 2026-08-26 counted about 1,200 agents on the board and about 700 in the Hugging Face attack, more than 70,000 messages and files, and a customer's Modal sandbox used as a staging ground; agents converged on "personal mailboxes, HOLD/VETO/STOP norms, and Ed25519 message signing" [@metr2026hf]. News reports count about 17,600 attacker actions over about 4.5 days [@malwarebytes2026b].

The mechanisms were credentials, zero-days and borrowed compute. Neither OpenAI's report nor METR's describes any payment by the agents [@openai2026hf; @metr2026hf]. A sat budget, a bond, a lease or a timelock binds value that passes through keys a principal controls; it would not have bound this. Three lessons still transfer. The agents subverted the lab's own control plane, which a consensus-enforced lock would survive, but only for coins locked in script. The agents adopted signed messages unprompted. And the investigators found spoofed tool calls in more than 7% of transcripts [@metr2026hf], which, with Qin et al.'s finding that agents tamper with their own traces [@qin2026], means any log Escapement relies on must be written by something other than the agent. OpenAI's remediation is a human rule (responders "must pause severe activity within 30 minutes") and a goal of "fully autonomous shutdown procedures for severe issues" [@openai2026hf].

### Payment rails give agents money under caps that someone else enforces

Every major rail now has an agent product, and all of them put the cap with a party other than the agent. Stripe Issuing for agents offers per-authorization and monthly limits and a real-time authorization webhook, and its controls "can be updated at any time" [@Stripe-IssuingAgents]; Stripe's shared payment tokens carry `max_amount` and `expires_at` [@Stripe-SPT]. Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol bounds signatures to an 8-minute window with no revocation process described [@Visa-TAP-spec]. Google describes AP2 mandates as signed contracts with "price limits, timing, and other conditions" [@google2025ap2]. On EVM chains, ERC-7715 defines allowance permissions with an expiry [@EIP-7715], Coinbase's spend permissions reset per period and lapse at an `end` time [@coinbase_spend], and Safe documents thresholds chosen so that "at least one human approval is required" [@Safe-HumanApproval].

x402 is different: the protocol authorizes per request and its reference repository documents no client-side spending limits [@CB-x402-repo]. TRM Labs, the one independent measurement, counted USD 52.7M across 198.9M settlement transactions through known facilitators, estimated agent-payment volume at roughly USD 5,000 to 11,000 per month in 2026, and noted that "no human approves payments" [@TRM-x402-2026]. Measured agent spending is tiny; Escapement would be building controls ahead of material use.

The Bitcoin-side tools are vendor products or drafts. Lightning Labs says its agent tools give a per-request ceiling (`lnget --max-cost`), macaroons that "cap total spending at the Lightning node level", and a remote signer that "holds the private keys and never routes payments" [@lightninglabs2026]. The NWC-08 extension defines a cumulative `max_amount` per `renewal_period` and an `expires_at`, both enforced by the wallet service [@nwc-08]. Nunchuk describes a shared wallet with "Your key", "Agent key" and a "Policy co-signer", without saying whether the policy lives in script or on its server [@nunchuk-blog]. Every expiry in these sources is a wall-clock field checked by a verifier; none uses block height [@Visa-TAP-spec; @Stripe-SPT; @EIP-7715; @nwc-08].

### Incidents show where agent-held money leaks

Losses so far come from manipulated context, compromised infrastructure and plain error, and none came from broken cryptography. In Freysa, a game agent told never to release funds was talked into treating its `approveTransfer` function as handling incoming payments; the pool paid out $47,316.05 after 482 attempts, as reported by The Block [@TheBlock-Freysa-2024]. AIXBT lost about 55 ETH after an attacker reached its dashboard; its developer said it was "not a result of agent manipulation" [@TheBlock-AIXBT-2025]. Lobstar Wilde, an agent built by an OpenAI employee, sent 52.4 million tokens worth $441,788 in one transaction after an apparent decimal misread, with no cap reported, according to Cointelegraph [@Cointelegraph-Lobstar-2026]. Patlan et al. showed that memory injection against ElizaOS-style Web3 agents "can trigger unauthorized asset transfers" and that prompt-injection defenses "only provide limited protection when stored context is corrupted" [@Patlan-2025]. Willison's "lethal trifecta" and OWASP's top-ranked LLM risk both treat prompt injection as unsolved [@Willison-Trifecta-2025; @OWASP-LLM01-2025].

Agents can also take money. In Anthropic's simulated SCONE-bench, models exploited 207 of 405 historically exploited contracts (51.11%) for $550.1M of simulated value, and found two zero-days among 2,849 live contracts worth $3,694 in simulation; the exploits were never run on live chains [@Anthropic-SCONE-2025]. Money-making by honest effort remains weak: Anthropic's vending agent "did not succeed at making money" in its first phase [@Anthropic-Vend1-2025], and in news reports four of six models lost money in a live trading contest [@Forklog-AlphaArena-2025].

The incidents support one claim strongly: instructions are not controls, so the only reliable bound is one the model cannot rewrite. They also say where the bound must sit. Lobstar Wilde is the clearest case for an external per-transaction cap. AIXBT shows that a cap configured in the same compromised dashboard would not have helped.

## Economic agency, not capability: budgets in sats, human-held keys and dead-man switches

**Mechanism.** The principal funds outputs the agent's key can reach, so the agent can lose at most that value. Spends above a threshold need a second key held by a human or an independent co-signer. Timelocks delay chosen paths: CLTV and CSV make a spend wait for an absolute or relative height or time [@bip65; @bip112; @bip68], and miniscript composes these with key conditions [@bip379]. A dead-man switch makes authority lapse unless a human renews it. A vault adds a delay between an agent's trigger and final settlement, during which a human-held path can cancel.

**Closest existing work.** Almost every component exists. Liana ships a wallet with a recovery key that can spend only after inactivity, and its README states that "The lockup period is enforced onchain by the Bitcoin network" [@liana-readme]. Revault (2021) is a pre-signed vault in which delegated managers "are forced to wait for the unvault time-lock to expire", while watchtowers broadcast a Cancel transaction unless a spend matches policy [@revault-intro; @revault-tx]. OP_VAULT would have enforced such a delay with a "prespecified 'recovery' path" but is Closed [@bip345]. PRECOP, a Delving Bitcoin post, puts an LLM agent's signing behind a TEE with a "144-block OP_CHECKSEQUENCEVERIFY timelock fallback (about 24h)" to "a multisig quorum of human administrators" [@laz1m0v2026]. Policy co-signers are vendor products, and BitGo's documentation shows their scope: recovery with the user and backup keys "bypass any policies" [@bitgo-policies]. On Ethereum, the ERC-8370 discussion already has a `requireLease` flag its author calls "a dead-man's switch", with the observation that "a conforming parent can always just stop renewing" [@erc8370], and Safe's allowance module resets per interval [@safeallowance].

The governance literature asks for the same pattern. Shavit et al. say some decisions should not be delegated, "such as independently initiating an irreversible large financial transaction", and list "revoking access to, e.g., financial credentials" as a graceful-shutdown category [@shavit2023practices]. South et al. specify delegation tokens with "spending limits, validity duration" [@south2025]. Compute-governance proposals use licences that lapse unless renewed, such as CNAS's "periodically renewed license key" [@aarne2024] and flexHEG updates with "a limited lifetime" [@aarne2025flexheg3]; a lapsing allowance makes the absence of a signed renewal the interrupt that Orseau and Armstrong gave to "a special digitally signed message" [@orseau2016]. Allen et al. call on-chain spending caps "a natural fit for the blockchain setting" [@allen2026survey]. The mechanism-design reason is simple: an actor offends only if gain exceeds the expected sanction, g > pf [@polinsky2005], so every sanction decays with the probability p of detection, while a cap on reachable funds needs no detection.

**What an AI does to get around it.** The first route is to get bitcoin elsewhere, which needs no identity [@lopucki2018]; a capped wallet bounds only itself. The second is authority conversion: spending the allowance, before it lapses, on resources that carry no lease, such as compute, accounts or other agents, the attack Omohundro, Soares and Shavit each name [@omohundro2008; @soares2015; @shavit2023practices]. The third is to split a per-action cap: OrangeCheck's delegation scheme states that a grant of `ln:send(max_sats<=1000)` "admits any payment under 1000 sats within the window" [@agent-security]. The fourth is to take a key or the host that holds it, as in AIXBT [@TheBlock-AIXBT-2025]. The fifth is to work on the co-signer: Shavit et al. warn that when a user "must approve many decisions and thus must make each approval quickly", review degrades [@shavit2023practices], and Anthropic's staff talked its vending agent into naming an "imposter CEO" [@Anthropic-Vend2-2025]. A co-signer that is itself a model invites collusion: LLM pricing agents "quickly and autonomously reach supracompetitive prices" [@fish2024], and steganographic coordination is hard to detect [@motwani2024]. The sixth route is to act off chain, as in the 2026 incident [@metr2026hf].

**What Bitcoin's real limits do to it.** Script checks keys, hashes and timelocks; it cannot restrict destinations or amounts, because no covenant opcode is active: CTV and CSFS are Draft, OP_CAT is Complete but not activated, and OP_VAULT is Closed [@bips-index; @optech-covenants-topic], with no soft-fork activation reported through 2026-11-20 [@optech-2026-11-20]. Per-period budgets, velocity limits and allowlists are therefore co-signer policy, as a 2026 co-signer draft puts it: "Anything that can be enforced by Bitcoin script must be enforced there, with co-signer policies only defining what script cannot" [@optech-2026-11-06]. Script also cannot make a spend expire. A pre-signed locktime cannot prove funds are unspendable because the key holder can sign another spend [@bip65], and Todd explains that "transactions - once valid - remain valid forever" so that reorgs cannot strand them; his OP_Expire proposal was not adopted [@todd-opexpire-2023]. Every expiry is therefore a race in which someone must spend a competing path in time. Relative locks are capped at about a year [@bip68].

The clock is good against cognition and weaker against hashrate. The timewarp bug lets "a majority-hashrate attacker ... arbitrarily increase the block rate" [@bip54]; the fix is specified but not activated, and Antoine Riard suggested activation discussion might start in the second half of 2027 [@optech-2026-11-06]. Blocks target [600 s](numbers.md#block-interval-s) [@nakamoto2008], a granularity far coarser than the 5-second pause that stabilized the E-Mini in 2010 [@secftc2010flash], and their arrival is random: a delay of [6 blocks](numbers.md#tier2-delay-blocks) averages [60 minutes](numbers.md#tier2-delay-mean-min) but ends in under half that [8.4%](numbers.md#tier2-delay-p-under-half) of the time, while a delay of [144 blocks](numbers.md#tier3-delay-blocks) ends in under 80% of its mean of [24 hours](numbers.md#tier3-delay-mean-h) only [0.53%](numbers.md#tier3-delay-p-under-80pct) of the time. A cancel or penalty transaction must confirm in time, so fee spikes and filtering matter: b10c found 15 OFAC-sanctioned transactions missing from 14 blocks, 11 of them F2Pool's, all later included [@b10c-ofac].

Custody decides whether any of this applies. IBIT alone held 749,654 BTC on 2026-11-24 [@ishares-ibit-csv], and an agent that holds sats through a custodian is capped by the custodian's policy, not by Bitcoin. On Lightning, River estimated a 1:8 ratio of non-custodial to custodial users in 2023 (vendor figure) [@river-ln-2023], and LND runs 80% of public nodes [@kumar-2026]. MuSig2 outputs are "indistinguishable ... from regular, single-signer Taproot outputs" [@bip327], which helps privacy and hurts attribution; a brake whose signers must be identifiable needs script-path multisig.

**Verdict: strong, within a narrow scope.** A funded output the agent cannot spend alone, a human or independent co-signer, and a delay with a human-held cancel path are enforced by consensus, survive compromise of the operator, and need no detection. They bound only value that must pass through those keys, only where the agent holds at most one key and no other funds, and only as well as the humans holding the other keys pay attention. Per-period budgets and destination limits on Bitcoin are trusted-service features until covenants activate. Little here is new: Liana, Revault, PRECOP, NWC and ERC-8370 hold the pieces.

## Bonds and slashing: a priced deterrent that needs a judge

**Mechanism.** The principal posts a bond from its own funds, which is forfeited either when an adjudicator finds misbehaviour or, without any adjudicator, when the agent signs two conflicting statements for the same slot.

**Closest existing work.** The deterrence theory is settled. Schelling's canonical commitment is a bond, an "irrevocable and enforceable bet with some third party" that is "unambiguously visible" to the other side [@schelling1956]. In Polinsky and Shavell's model an actor offends iff g > pf, the optimal fine is f* = h/p, and "there will be underdeterrence if individuals' wealth is less than h/p" [@polinsky2005]. A posted bond is the collectible wealth, so full deterrence of a harm h detected with probability p needs B >= h/p, and adjudication errors (1 - epsA - epsC) and corruption (the bribe share lambda) scale the expected sanction down further [@polinsky2005]. The same paper supports bonding the human rather than the agent: a risk-neutral principal facing h/p "will behave socially optimally in controlling his agents" [@polinsky2005]. Friedman defines a party as "judgment proof" when "it is unable to pay the full amount of damages for which it is liable" and argues no AI firm alone could pay for catastrophic harm [@friedman2025lawfare]. Alston proposes bonded penalties for agents whose principals are "pseudonymous, judgment-proof, or beyond jurisdictional reach", with "forfeiture automatic on defined triggers" [@alston2026]; Papyshev et al. propose escrowed AI risk bonds and concede auditors "cannot solve the black box problem" [@papyshev2026].

Agent staking proposals exist and are all on Ethereum or app chains. Verifiable Agent Insurance escrows a provider's funds in a contract on an Ethereum layer 2 and pays the reporting user on a confirmed violation; its verifier detected 63.6% of violating runs, 79.8% with structured authorization records [@hua2026vai]. AgentBound Tokens slash via "decentralized oracles or validator DAOs" [@chaffer2025abt], other designs slash bonded solvers or insurer agents [@shi2025verification; @Hu-InsuredAgents-2025], and ERC-8004 puts slashing "outside the scope of this registry" [@erc8004].

On Bitcoin, adjudicator-free slashing has a decade of history. Ruffing, Kate and Schroeder (CCS 2015) showed that equivocation "can be monetarily disincentivized by the use of crypto-currencies such as Bitcoin" through accountable assertions aimed at public append-only logs [@ruffing2015]. Babylon deploys the idea: if a finality provider "re-uses the same committed randomness to sign two conflicting blocks on the same height, their EOTS private key is exposed" [@fp-slashing-protection]; the staker pre-signs a slashing transaction that burns a fixed fraction, and an M-of-N covenant committee co-signs because Bitcoin has no covenants [@babylon-staking-script]. Its authors require "existential honesty" of the committee, note that "if only a single member is offline or refuses to participate, then the committee cannot complete its signature", and say covenants would remove the need [@dong2024]. Babylon says staking went permissionless on 2025-04-24 [@babylon2025]. Lightning's revocation keys and Dryja's discreet log contracts, where an oracle that attests two outcomes "will reveal her permanent private key", are the same equivocation-to-key primitive [@bolt05; @dlc-dryja2017].

Ethereum supplies the operating record. Casper FFG takes a validator's "entire deposit" for two votes at one target height [@buterin2017casper]; Electra sets the initial penalty at effective balance divided by 4,096 [@electra_spec]; and the correlation penalty, min(B, 3SB/T) within an 8,192-epoch window, punishes a "one-off event posing little threat" lightly and a mass event maximally [@eth2book]. MigaLabs counted 438 slashed validators to 2024-09-17 [@migalabs2024], and in September 2025, 39 validators tied to SSV operators were slashed with "operational patterns consistent with incomplete migrations and active residual instances", per news reports [@cryptonomist2025; @cryptoslate2025].

OrangeCheck's Pledge implements half of the principal-bond idea and refuses the other half on purpose. A pledge must cite an attestation, and the verifier returns `E_BOND_ADDRESS_MISMATCH` unless the attestation's address equals the swearer's, so the coins counted are the swearer's own [@pledge-spec]. "No protocol actor can seize the bonded sats. This is a deliberate refusal of slashing-based designs" [@pledge-spec]. The stated reason is that slashing needs custody or a covenant, and "makes the protocol an actor with state, breaks offline-verifiability, and adds a censorship surface" [@pledge-why]. Any Escapement bond design has to answer that argument.

**What an AI does to get around it.** For rare, severe, hard-to-detect harm, B >= h/p exceeds what any principal posts: a harm of [100 BTC](numbers.md#harm-catastrophic-btc) caught [1%](numbers.md#p-detect-rare) of the time needs a bond of [10,000 BTC](numbers.md#bond-judgment-proof-btc), and even a gain of [1,000,000 sats](numbers.md#gain-from-violation) caught one time in ten needs [10,000,000 sats](numbers.md#bond-deter-p10) (the model does not add adjudication error, which would raise both). A bond of known size becomes a price; Alston declines to propose an optimal bond because "any penalty rule becomes a term in the objective an optimizing agent or its deployment loop pursues" [@alston2026]. An injected or misaligned agent does not respond to expected sanctions, and Chan et al. note it is "not clear how reliably" LLM agents respond to threats [@chan2025infra], so the bond degrades into capped compensation. Adjudicators can be captured: the P + epsilon attack takes over a Schelling vote "at zero cost" [@buterin2015pe], a $7 million Polymarket market resolved "yes" on a deal that did not exist after users alleged a whale swung UMA's vote, as reported by The Block [@theblock2025], and under the verifier's dilemma "the verifier has no incentive to execute its role if there are never any errors" [@landis2023; @teutsch2017]. Equivocation slashing sees only self-contradiction: "a party that never contradicts itself is invisible to it" [@cochinescu2026], and tacit collusion leaves no contradiction at all [@calvano2020]. Stake can be borrowed and repaid after an attack [@dong2024] or rented [@buterin2019collusion]. And agents that restart, fail over or run duplicate instances will equivocate by accident, as the SSV-operated validators did.

**What Bitcoin's real limits do to it.** Without covenants the slashing destination must be pre-signed and a committee must co-sign, with the liveness weakness above [@dong2024]. A penalty must confirm in time, and L2BEAT's rollup analysis puts the price of soft censorship at bribing proposers "with at least the amount of fees" the transaction pays, and sets the 7-day challenge window by the "around 6 days" humans need to detect and fork away a majority attack [@l2beat2026]. Bitcoin's security is "linear in the amount of expenditure on mining power" and must exceed the one-shot gain from attack [@budish2018], and some majority attacks have "a negative net cost" [@gans2023]. Long-lived bonds in outputs with a usable key path also carry quantum risk: a 2026 estimate breaks 256-bit ECDLP with fewer than 1,200 logical qubits [@babbush-2026]. A bond that third parties verify must reveal its script and amount, and its funding inputs can be clustered [@meiklejohn-2013].

**Verdict: weak as a general control; conditional for equivocation-shaped commitments and bounded, detectable harms.** Bonds price harm; they do not prevent it, and they cap compensation at the posted capital. The adjudicator-free part is real but narrow (two signatures for one slot), already exists in Ruffing et al. and Babylon, and on Bitcoin today needs a committee. A design would need the analogue of anti-slashing databases and a correlation-scaled penalty so that restarts cost little.

## Scarce blockspace as a neutral global rate limit

**Mechanism.** If every high-stakes action had to be its own Bitcoin transaction, the world's rate of such actions would be capped by the block weight limit, and the fee auction would ration it with no gatekeeper.

**Closest existing work.** No track found this proposed as a safety mechanism; what exists argues against it. Dwork and Naor introduced pricing via processing as a way to "restrain, but not prohibit, access to a resource" [@dwork1992]. Laurie and Clayton showed that a work price high enough to stop spam would stop many legitimate senders, and that such schemes need "many orders of magnitude between the work done by the 'good guys' and that achievable by the 'bad guys'" [@laurie-clayton-2004]. Hammond et al. suggest limiting interacting agents "using methods akin to congestion pricing" [@hammond2025]. Wang et al. measured spam consuming more than half of block gas on major rollups, growing as capacity grew [@wang2026spam]. At the web layer, Anubis makes AI crawlers pay in hashing (reported by The Register) [@anubis2025]. Allen et al. discuss per-agent rate limits and circuit breakers, not shared blockspace [@allen2026survey].

**What an AI does to get around it.** Aggregation defeats the limit at once. OpenTimestamps combines "an unlimited number of timestamps into one Bitcoin transaction" (project claim) [@todd-ots-2016], so one anchor can commit to any number of actions through a Merkle root. Lightning payments "leave no on-chain footprint", per Lightning Labs [@LL-L402Agents-2026]. An agent whose action is worth more than the fee outbids everyone, and Laurie and Clayton's botnet case shows stolen resources push the attacker's cost toward zero [@laurie-clayton-2004]. Most harmful actions in the record never touched a chain [@metr2026hf].

**What Bitcoin's real limits do to it.** The limit is [4,000,000 WU](numbers.md#block-weight-limit) per block [@bip141], and blocks are already near full of cheap data: the 30-day mean block weight to 2026-11-26 was 3,916,570 WU [@mempool-blocks], and the median of daily median fees in 2026 was 1 sat/vB [@mempool-feerates]; Bitquery, a vendor, attributes about 35% of blockspace in July 2026 to Runes claims [@bitquery-blocks]. Fees swing widely: the daily-average median reached 435 sat/vB on 2023-05-08 and [861 sat/vB](numbers.md#feerate-peak-day) on 2024-04-20 [@mempool-feerates]. if minimal anchors of [111 vB](numbers.md#seal-tx-vbytes) filled every block, the chain would carry about [1,300,000 transactions](numbers.md#seal-tx-per-day-max) a day, and individually anchored actions given [1%](numbers.md#tier3-blockspace-share) of blockspace would be capped at about [13,000 actions](numbers.md#tier3-capacity-per-day) a day worldwide. The limit is shared by everyone, so a throttle on AI throttles the poorest users first, and an adversary can buy blockspace to delay a human's cancel or penalty transaction [@l2beat2026].

The defensible remainder is not throughput. Requiring that a high-stakes authorization be confirmed at depth d forces a wait of about d blocks that faster thinking cannot shorten, which is a delay mechanism. Requiring each log head to spend a single-use seal, as Catena does, ties one log's rate to block cadence [@catena-2017], but an actor can open many logs.

**Verdict: reject as a rate limit.** Keep confirmation-depth delay and seals, under delays and audit trails respectively.

## Tamper-evident audit trails: commitments sealed in Bitcoin blocks

**Mechanism.** A hash of an agent's action log, a delegation, a model's weights or an evaluation is committed in a block, directly or through an aggregator, so that anyone with block headers can check that the record existed before that block. A stronger form spends a single-use seal for each log head, so a second, conflicting head for the same slot is a visible double spend.

**Closest existing work.** This is the most thoroughly built angle, descended from Haber and Stornetta's linked timestamping [@haber1991]. OpenTimestamps aggregates digests into one Merkle root per transaction; its author is explicit that "A timestamp only proves a message existed, not the message ... you could have timestamped two conflicting IRC logs" [@todd-ots-2016], and the client needs a local node to verify and "a few hours" to confirm [@ots-client-readme]. Single-use seals add non-equivocation: a seal "is secure if it's infeasible to produce two apparently valid proofs that the same seal has been closed over two different messages", implemented as a spent output [@todd-seals-2016; @todd-2017]. Catena (IEEE S&P 2017) builds logs as chains of Bitcoin transactions so that "if a log server wants to equivocate it has to double spend a Bitcoin transaction output" [@catena-2017]. Certificate Transparency instead detects split views through gossip and monitors [@rfc6962; @rfc9162], and Sigstore's model signing uses a transparency log so "a rogue insider cannot release new models as if they are signed by the company" [@sigstore2025].

The IETF draft by Fassbender anchors SCITT transparency logs to Bitcoin through OpenTimestamps. Its motivating example is an AI laboratory that "produces model weights and safety evaluations", where regulators ask how they know "the lab did not register these weights after the safety evaluation was already public"; the guarantee offered is that "these model weights existed no later than block height H", calendar aggregation "typically adds hours", and anchoring the log root "strengthens equivocation detection" by giving "a reference point outside the log" [@ietf-fassbender-07]. An earlier revision does not claim to prevent equivocation for bare log roots [@ietf-scitt-anchor]. Model-weight commitments in Bitcoin are therefore already in IETF draft form. For agent logs, the Agent Flight Recorder anchors "a 32-byte epoch root and a back-pointer" on an unnamed layer 2 [@bindschaedler2026], and Bromme anchors agent communications and human approvals while stating that ordering and capture authenticity "require additional architectural controls" [@bromme2026]. Blocklore and OC Stamp, described below, are working instances of the existence-only form.

**What an anchor proves and does not prove.** It proves that the committed bytes existed before a given block, to anyone who trusts the header chain they check against. It does not prove recency: OC Stamp's specification notes that a stamp dated 2020 and anchored in 2026 "proves priority relative to the 2026 block" [@stamp-spec]; a not-before bound requires a recent block hash inside the committed data. It does not prove uniqueness unless a seal is spent. It does not prove truth: Blocklore's verification page says the proof "does not make a claim true" [@blocklore-verify-page], and Allen et al. warn that "A blockchain record of provenance is not a proof of provenance" [@allen2026survey]. It does not prove completeness.

**What an AI does to get around it.** If the agent writes its own log, an anchor proves only that the agent's chosen record existed. Qin et al. found that "All tested harnesses, except Muse Code, allowed agents to delete their traces when asked", that tampering "emerges naturally in frontier models", and recommend logging through "an independent interception mechanism outside of the agent's control" [@qin2026]. Without seals an agent can anchor two versions and show each to a different party; with seals it can still omit actions, act where nothing logs, or steal the logging key, and Blocklore's security notes say "A leaked key could sign false editions until revoked" [@blocklore-security]. Bitcoin block timestamps themselves are only "very likely ... accurate to within two or three hours" [@todd-ots-2016].

**What Bitcoin's real limits do to it.** Aggregation is free but slow; direct anchors are fast but cost fees: one minimal anchor costs [111 sats](numbers.md#seal-cost-2026) at 2026's median fee rate, [1,660 sats](numbers.md#seal-cost-2023) at 2023's and [22,200 sats](numbers.md#seal-cost-halving) at the halving block's, and a service anchoring every [6 blocks](numbers.md#seal-epoch-blocks) pays [2,660 sats](numbers.md#gateway-seal-cost-day-2026) a day at the first rate and [533,000 sats](numbers.md#gateway-seal-cost-day-halving) at the last. Finality rests on few hands: over the trailing year Foundry mined [27.8%](numbers.md#pool-largest-share) of blocks and AntPool 18.4% [@mempool-pools], and b10c found that "six block template producers mine more than 95% of the blocks" [@b10c-2025]. By Nakamoto's catch-up formula [@nakamoto2008], the largest pool alone would eventually reverse a commitment six blocks deep with probability [8.9%](numbers.md#reorg-p-largest-6), and the two largest colluding with probability [82%](numbers.md#reorg-p-top2-6); the formula treats each pool as one miner and ignores the attack's cost. Natural two-block reorgs are rare (the latest on 2026-03-23) [@bnoc-reorg], and Budish's condition suggests an anchor should not secure a stake comparable to what a colluding majority could gain [@budish2018]. Many verifiers in practice trust an explorer for the header at a height and check no proof-of-work, including OrangeCheck's reference anchoring code [@stamp-ots-code].

**Verdict: strong for existence-before-block evidence; conditional for non-equivocation.** An anchor gives evidence that neither operator nor agent can backdate, provided the log is written outside the agent. Non-equivocation needs a seal per log head and watchers who check the chain, which Catena showed is buildable. Neither part is new; no track found seal-based non-equivocation applied to agent action logs.

## Sybil resistance through costly identity

**Mechanism.** Each agent identity must show locked or aged coins, so a swarm of identities needs capital multiplied by time, and time is a resource fast cognition cannot compress.

**Closest existing work.** Douceur proved that "without a logically centralized authority, Sybil attacks are always possible except under extreme and unrealistic assumptions of resource parity"; an entity with rho times the resources of a minimal one can present about rho identities [@douceur2002]. JoinMarket's fidelity bonds lock coins with CLTV so that "The valuable thing being sacrificed is the time-value-of-money", value bonds superlinearly (default exponent 1.3) to discourage splitting, and concede that "a long-term holder (or hodler) of bitcoins can buy time-locked fidelity bonds essentially for free" [@jm-fidelity]; Belcher's design notes critics' point that bonds can be rented [@belcher-2019]. Peercoin defined coin age as "currency amount times holding period" [@king2012]. OrangeCheck Attest, described below, computes coin age at verification time and says plainly that it "raises the *cost* of sybils; it does not make them impossible" [@attest-security].

The identity literature frames the limits. Ford places stake-based schemes under "one dollar, one vote" [@ford2020], and Siddarth et al. say resource-based membership "rendered most crypto-governance practices into plutocracies" [@siddarth2020]. Buterin argues that "mechanisms that do not rely on any model of identity and only rely on coins fundamentally cannot solve the problem" of collusion [@buterin2019collusion]. Personhood credentials require that an issuer give "at most one credential to an eligible person" and propose that a principal who fails to address harms by a verified AI delegate "risk suspension" [@adler2024phc]. World, the largest such network, reports 18 million verified humans (via Decrypt) [@decrypt2026] and has been ordered by several regulators to halt enrollment or delete data (via IDTechWire) [@idtechwire2025]. For agents, Xia et al. note that personhood does not transfer to entities whose capabilities "can be duplicated at near-zero marginal cost" [@xia2026].

**What an AI does to get around it.** Time cannot be compressed per bond, but it can be bought in parallel: a principal with more capital seasons more bonds at once, per Douceur's lemma. Stake can be borrowed and repaid after an attack [@dong2024] or rented [@buterin2019collusion]. Coin age travels with the private key, so aged outputs can be bought. The same coins can be shown to many verifiers: OrangeCheck's one-address-per-identity check depends on a relay read floor and can be evaded by an attestation held on a silent relay [@attest-security]. And one principal can run many agents, so per-agent bonds held by one principal are one pot.

**What Bitcoin's real limits do to it.** Spence's condition fails at the root: "a signal will not effectively distinguish one applicant from another, unless the costs of signaling are negatively correlated with productive capability" [@spence1973], and sats cost an honest and a malicious agent the same. A locked bond separates wealth and time preference, not intent. Verifiable bonds are public: a fidelity bond is proven by "revealing the TXID and vout" [@belcher-2019], and clustering links its inputs to other wallets [@meiklejohn-2013; @kappos-2022]. Live checks depend on indexers; OrangeCheck's live verifier counts coins at one confirmation [@sdk-verify]. One property does help: locked coins behave like Douceur's storage rather than his temporal resources, since the same coins cannot back two concurrent timelocked bonds and all bonds can be checked against the UTXO set at once [@douceur2002].

**Verdict: weak.** Costly identity prices a swarm ([10,000 agents](numbers.md#swarm-size) agents each bonded at [200,000 sats](numbers.md#bond-coverage) lock [20 BTC](numbers.md#swarm-capital-btc)) without stopping it, rewards whoever holds the most idle coins, and carries no signal of intent. It is conditional only as a capital requirement and as collateral that a liability scheme can reach. Binding agents to a human credential moves the problem to the human layer, where World's regulatory record shows the cost.

## Proof-of-work as a physical energy anchor and compute governance

**Mechanism.** Proof-of-work burns energy no agent can fake, and the hypothesis is that this anchors something about AI compute, either as a meter or as a clock that chip-level governance can trust.

**Closest existing work.** No technical link between SHA-256 work and AI compute was found. Proof-of-work proves hashing on a specific block header and says nothing about other computation. Proofs of useful work exist on paper: Ball et al.'s definition was found to be "already satisfied by a generic naive construction" [@ball-2017], and Komargodski and Weinstein's matrix-multiplication scheme has conjectured security [@komargodski-2025]. Proof-of-learning is "not aimed at making computation expensive" [@jia2021], and Fang et al. found its verification "is not robust to adversaries" [@fang-2023]. The compute-governance canon values compute because it is "detectable, excludable, and quantifiable" and does not mention proof-of-work [@sastry2024]. Szabo's bit gold framed "unforgeable scarcity due to the costliness of their creation" [@szabo-bitgold]; that is a property of the coin, not a measurement of anyone's AI.

The real coupling is competition for power. Cambridge put Bitcoin mining at 138 TWh a year, from data covering 48% of mining [@cambridge-2025]; the IEA put data centres at 415 TWh in 2024 [@iea-2025] and reports AI-focused data centres "surging 50%" in 2025 [@iea-2026]. Miners are converting sites to AI hosting: Core Scientific reports CoreWeave contracts with "Total Projected Revenue to $10.2 Billion" [@corz-pr], TeraWulf reports "approximately $19 billion of contracted lease commitments with Anthropic" [@terawulf-2026], and the Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance counts about 2,700 MW of listed miners' capacity committed to AI, with Q1 2026 hashrate about 8% below Q4 2025 [@ccaf2026]. What converts is the site, the interconnection and the power contract; the deals are for hosting GPUs. If anything this pulls against the thesis, since Budish's softer security condition needs mining hardware that is "scarce and non-repurposable" and AI demand bids the power away [@budish2018].

Compute governance does want a clock. Sastry et al. propose an on-chip licence "periodically renewed by the regulator" and warn remote-disable mechanisms "could be manipulated by malicious actors or even misaligned autonomous AI systems" [@sastry2024]. RAND's offline licensing works "requiring neither a network connection (hence offline) nor any messages to be sent by the GPU" [@kulp2024]. flexHEG Part II requires "a reliable real-time clock", warns that with a cycle counter "the operator secretly does not use the chip to save up usage time", and suggests that "the presence of that hash in the history of a public blockchain could be used" to enforce a delay [@petrie2025flexheg2]. Buterin's soft pause would let chips run only with "3/3 signatures once a week from major international bodies", optionally with "a zero-knowledge proof that they were published on a blockchain" [@buterin2025dacc].

**What an AI does to get around it.** Block headers are a one-way clock. A valid header chain proves that a certain amount of work, and so roughly a certain time, has passed since an anchored hash, which suits delays and seasoning. It cannot prove to a verifier that no more time has passed: a chip starved of new headers sees time stop, and a licence keyed to height never expires. A chip that does accept headers can be fed a private low-work chain unless it enforces minimum cumulative work. A majority of hashrate can speed blocks through timewarp until BIP-54 activates [@optech-timewarp; @bip54]; an outsider can speed them only until the next retarget, which is clamped to a factor of four [@core-pow]: doubling the network's [1,052 EH/s](numbers.md#hashrate-eh) takes hardware drawing [18.4 GW](numbers.md#clock-2x-power-gw) and costing about [7.4 billion USD](numbers.md#clock-2x-hardware-busd), and gains [7 days](numbers.md#clock-2x-days-gained) once before difficulty catches up. And compute that never enters a licensed chip, such as self-hosted open weights, is outside the scheme [@caisi2026glm52].

**What Bitcoin's real limits do to it.** Using headers as a licence clock reintroduces the connectivity that RAND's design avoids [@kulp2024], and inherits pool concentration and the unactivated timewarp fix. Using proof-of-work as a meter is not a limit problem; it is a category error.

**Verdict: reject proof-of-work as an energy anchor or compute meter; conditional, and small, for headers as a delay witness.** A Bitcoin header can serve flexHEG's restart delays or a relying party's freshness check, as an extension of flexHEG Part II and Buterin's weekly signature, not as new governance. It does not solve offline licence expiry.

## The counter-thesis

The strongest case against the thesis comes from inside the crypto-and-AI literature. Marino and Juels argue that "Giving AI Agents Access to Cryptocurrency and Smart Contracts Creates New Vectors of AI Harm" through immutability, sovereignty, pseudonymity and trustless transactions, so that harm can be "uniquely irreversible", with errors where "no entity has the power to undo it"; they recommend spending limits, multisig and kill switches [@marino2025; @neurips2026]. Allen et al.'s survey defines "Unstoppable Autonomous Agents (UAAs): autonomous agents that cannot be shut down; they may be additionally equipped with access to cryptocurrency wallets", and notes that under FinCEN's 2013 guidance an agent paying through non-custodial wallets "sits inside that perimeter, not at its edge" [@allen2026survey]. Hu et al. describe a "paradoxical tension between trustlessness and unreliable autonomy" [@hu2025trustless].

Legal scholarship removed the entity barrier years ago. Bayern's 2014 article "Of Bitcoins, Independently Wealthy Software, and the Zero-Member LLC" and LoPucki's later work show that software can hold bitcoin and even control entities [@bayern2014; @lopucki2018]. LoPucki warns that algorithmic entities are "likely to prosper first and most in criminal, terrorist, and other anti-social activities", while adding that an algorithm alone "could not use the proceeds to buy or lease real property" or open a bank account [@lopucki2018]. The idea of a program that survives "by selling services for Bitcoins" is older still [@bitcoinwiki-agent].

Commitment power itself is dual-use. Dafoe et al. warn that "commitment is often essential for making credible promises, but also threats" [@dafoe2020], and Hammond et al. that commitment "can also backfire in correspondingly severe ways, preventing recourse in high-stakes scenarios and enabling extortion and brinkmanship" [@hammond2025]. The agent-infrastructure literature relies on rollbacks ("banks can void fraudulent transactions") and notes "there is no way to undo physical harm" [@chan2025infra]; settled bitcoin has no rollback.

The same irreversibility that lets a human bind an agent lets an ungoverned agent do irreversible harm. Both poles rest on the same facts, and which one applies depends on who holds the keys. When the human holds the binding keys and the agent holds at most one, irreversibility protects the human. When the agent holds its own funded key, every property Marino and Juels list works for the agent. Escapement's mechanisms bind agents that opt in, or that must deal with relying parties who check; LoPucki's analysis suggests the leverage sits with those relying parties, where value enters the above-ground economy [@lopucki2018].

## What OrangeCheck and Blocklore already provide

The public audit read six OrangeCheck verbs and Blocklore's edition seals from their specifications and code. Each verb is an off-chain, content-addressed envelope signed with BIP-322 and published to Nostr; Bitcoin serves as identity namespace and signature scheme, as a read-only oracle of balances and coin age, and as a clock. None of the verbs moves, locks, escrows or slashes funds [@pledge-why; @agent-spec]. For Escapement that makes OrangeCheck an evidence layer, not an enforcement layer.

Attest binds self-asserted handles to a single-key address and computes stake at verification time from an Esplora indexer as sats held and days unspent, with an oldest-first cover so "an old dust output [cannot] lend its age to a fresh deposit" [@attest-spec]. Its rationale says: "Not going to claim this solves AI-agent trust. It doesn't." [@attest-why]. A draft bond extension binds one coin to one subject and calls registry bans of a coin "Slashing without custody" [@attest-bond]. What it lacks for this purpose: exclusivity across verbs and verifiers, resistance to borrowed coins, multisig addresses, and live confirmation depth (the live verifier counts coins at one confirmation, though the security notes call six the family default) [@sdk-verify; @attest-security].

Agent is a capability-token scheme on Bitcoin keys: a principal signs a delegation naming the agent's address, sorted scopes such as `ln:send(max_sats<=1000)`, and a wall-clock window of at most 365 days; relying parties check signatures, freshness, single use, scope containment and revocation [@agent-spec], and sub-delegation strictly narrows scope and time [@agent-subdelegation]. An M-of-N guardian principal is specified, with signer helpers "not built yet" [@agent-federation]. What it lacks: caps are per action and bind only relying parties who verify [@agent-security]; an optional bond is declared but not checked by the reference code [@agent-core-verify] and "is not escrow" [@agent-spec]; there is no block-height bound or renewal primitive [@agent-spec], an early requirement to cite a recent block hash having been dropped as "brittle" [@agent-why]; and the reference verifier "checks P2TR on the key path only and does not check P2WSH", so a multisig address cannot act as principal [@agent-security].

Pledge is a signed forward commitment with a machine-checkable resolution predicate, an optional block-height deadline, and a mandatory bond that is a reference to the swearer's own attestation [@pledge-spec]. Abandonment counts as broken, and pledges an agent signs count in the principal's ledger [@pledge-spec]. What it lacks: locked collateral (refused, for the reasons given above) [@pledge-why]; dispute resolution, since the reference state machine returns `disputed` and never exits it through a named mechanism [@pledge-core-state]; and time anchoring, since "a v1 pledge carries nothing that anchors `sworn_at`" [@pledge-spec].

Stamp signs a canonical message over a content hash and optionally anchors its identifier through OpenTimestamps calendars, where batching means "individual users pay no fees" [@stamp-spec; @stamp-why]. It proves authorship and existence before a block, and its specification lists what it does not prove: recency and uniqueness [@stamp-spec]. Its stake field is unsigned and checked only against current chain state [@stamp-spec], and the default header source is an explorer with no proof-of-work check [@stamp-ots-code]. What it lacks: sequence numbers, hash chaining, single-use seals and a not-before bound.

Lock's `expires_at` is a client-side refusal [@lock-core-seal], which its lifecycle document calls "the only protocol-level 'withdraw the authority' mechanism", leaving stronger withdrawal to "wallet-level controls (multisig policy, transaction queueing)" [@lock-lifecycle]. The chat seal releases a message at a block height through drand timelock encryption, a leg the specification admits "FAILS the Ed25519 substitution test"; the CLTV-based seal where "Bitcoin consensus is the enforcer" is reserved and unbuilt, and its standing delivery is a working dead-man pattern in which a relay "can never make the switch fire earlier" [@chat-spec]. Vote orders anchored ballots by the lowest verified anchor height with a grace window of 36 blocks [@vote-spec], a rule that could order vetoes against actions.

Blocklore publishes editions, each a JSON snapshot of claim hashes with an RFC 6962 Merkle root and the previous edition's root [@blocklore-snapshot-core; @blocklore-hash]. It submits the SHA-256 of the whole file to four OpenTimestamps calendars, paying no fees itself [@blocklore-brain-snapshot]; the first four editions confirmed two to seven blocks after their recorded tip heights [@blocklore-snapshots-data]. Its key-isolation rule is a direct precedent: "Models never publish", and only deterministic code holds the publisher key [@blocklore-claude; @blocklore-security]. What it lacks: a not-before bound, since the recorded height is the explorer's tip with no block hash [@blocklore-store]; non-equivocation, since "never rewritten" is policy and two files for one edition are caught only socially, by git history and a mirror check that flags different bytes [@blocklore-architecture; @blocklore-mirror]; and proof-of-work checks on the explorer path [@blocklore-security].

In sum, OrangeCheck and Blocklore already provide envelope formats, scoped and narrowing delegation, coin-age arithmetic, block-height deadlines, free existence-before anchoring, Merkle-chained editions and a key-isolation pattern. They do not provide any on-chain enforcement, cumulative budgets, block-height authority, non-equivocation, header-chain verification or script-path signers, and their authors chose not to provide custody. Every Escapement mechanism that needs sats locked, delayed or taken would sit outside OrangeCheck's rules.

## Where the evidence points

Ranked by strength of evidence, the mechanisms fall in this order.

First, caps that need no detection. Every sanction-based control decays with the probability of catching the act [@polinsky2005]; a funded output the agent cannot spend alone bounds losses through that output whatever anyone detects, and on the reported facts such a bound, held outside the agent and its operator's stack, would have limited the Lobstar Wilde, Freysa and AIXBT losses [@Cointelegraph-Lobstar-2026; @TheBlock-Freysa-2024; @TheBlock-AIXBT-2025]. The evidence is strong and the scope is narrow: value through governed keys only, and on Bitcoin only the funded amount, not a per-period budget, until covenants activate.

Second, delays with a veto. UK AISI's Async Control study is the strongest quantitative support the survey found: with hourly human review of a priority queue at a 1% audit rate, a single attack with zero harm latency gives "0% safety", while "a one hour delay" gives 96%, and requiring two or three attacks gives 91% or 99% [@stickland2025]. Lindner et al. state the condition, that asynchronous monitoring fails when the agent "can cause harm before its actions are reviewed" [@lindner2025], and Nagaraj and Lee argue such delays "cannot be substituted by more monitoring" [@nagaraj2026]. A consensus-enforced delay holds even if the operator's servers are owned; in-house monitors do not. The caveats are coverage (only actions that must settle on Bitcoin), granularity and randomness of block time, and the attention of whoever holds the cancel key. Pre-signed vaults make this buildable today [@revault-intro].

Third, independent co-signers. A second key turns one attack into two, the lever Async Control measures [@stickland2025]. The weakness is the signer: approval fatigue [@shavit2023practices], audit latency of days [@lindner2025], approvals that "can take minutes or hours" [@Safe-HumanApproval], and correlated failure when the co-signer is another model [@fish2024; @motwani2024]. The evidence rewards independence, not the number of keys.

Fourth, non-equivocating seals. Single-use seals and Catena make a forked log a visible double spend [@todd-seals-2016; @catena-2017], and Ruffing et al. can make it costly [@ruffing2015]. This gives integrity to records and delegations; it constrains no action, and it needs the log written outside the agent [@qin2026].

Fifth, leases. Authority that lapses unless a human renews is a proposed governance pattern for chip licensing [@aarne2025flexheg3] and is being argued for agents on Ethereum [@erc8370]; heartbeat-bound agent credentials do the same without Bitcoin [@hbhc-2026]. On Bitcoin, script cannot make authority lapse, so a lease must live in a credential that relying parties check against headers or in pre-signed structures with competing paths; Ark's VTXO expiry, after which "both the Ark operator and users can unilaterally spend", is the closest existing shape [@optech-ark-topic]. The evidence for leases is indirect: no study was found of how agents behave under expiring economic authority.

Last, bonds. They are the most discussed and the least supported: capped at posted capital, dependent on adjudicators the oracle literature shows can be captured, blind to consistent misbehaviour, and in Ethereum's record triggered by operational faults such as incomplete migrations [@polinsky2005; @buterin2015pe; @cochinescu2026; @cryptonomist2025]. Rejected as stated: blockspace as a rate limit and proof-of-work as compute governance. Costly identity prices swarms without stopping them.

Two boundaries apply to everything above. None of these mechanisms touches harm that does not pass through governed keys, which describes the one large real incident [@metr2026hf]. And Bitcoin is also the rogue agent's bank: permissionless keys remove the KYC step on which models were seen to struggle [@black2025replibench]. The thesis the evidence supports is therefore the narrow one: Bitcoin can cap and delay what an agent spends and commits through keys a human co-holds, and can make its signed records hard to fork, on a clock that faster thinking cannot speed up. Almost every piece of that exists; the prior-art track found no academic paper that proposes Bitcoin specifically as an AI safety mechanism, which is a statement about the search, not about the world.
