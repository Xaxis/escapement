## The reviewer's report

## Summary of the submission

The paper asks two questions. Can Bitcoin's scarcity, block clock and unforgeable costs help shape what AI agents are rewarded and trained for, and not only cap them? And would labs, firms, insurers and relying parties adopt a way of checking agents because it pays or protects them?

Its answer is Escapement. Each agent identity is a timelocked bond plus a "tick chain": a sequence of Taproot outputs, each spent by the next, each committing through a key tweak to a Merkle-sum root of the identity's forecasts, staked promises and requests for consequential actions. Five scoring rules turn this chain into standing: credit only what was committed before resolution, score silence as failure, keep one forecast per event, score in non-overlapping rounds against an independent baseline, and score from genesis. On this record the paper argues four things:

1. **Theorem 1.** Under a condition on the baseline (H0), the score, a product over rounds of weighted means of likelihood ratios, is a nonnegative martingale. Ville's inequality bounds the chance that an identity without edge ever reaches score λ by 1/λ, so faking with probability π needs about πλ bonded identities.
2. **Training.** A per-claim proper-score reward computed from the complete record cannot be selected after the fact by agent or lab, but only for events the agent cannot influence. On events it can influence, the same reward pays it to cause surprises.
3. **Adoption.** A one-sided unraveling result and a two-sided model in which adoption has a tipping point, removable by seed readers who read regardless of others. The claim that staying out becomes costly for harmful actors is withdrawn, because a corrected agent-based model shows them cycling through fresh identities at a profit.
4. **Enforcement.** A tick chain with per-identity rate limits and guardian vetoes, and a height-difference lease that bounds when a release can count without reading anyone's clock. Both are built and checked on regtest in 104 checks.

The paper is explicit that no mechanism or theorem is new, and that the contribution is an integration. It also reports at length what a red team and the regtest lab broke: a light proof of releases, chip clocks, and the routing-around claim.

## Strengths

- **Candour.** Few papers in this area report their own failed claims, show the counterexamples, and name falsification tests (section 12). The body of the paper is, for the most part, careful.
- **The round rule.** Averaging likelihood ratios within a round and multiplying across rounds is a correct and neat use of e-value averaging under dependence [@vovk2021values]. The correlated-claims and tail-risk counterexamples are instructive, and the link to Foster and Young's objection to track records [@foster2010gaming] is well drawn.
- **The regtest lab.** It is real engineering, reproducible from one command, and it found genuine defects that a Bitcoin reader will value: txid proofs that leave out the witness, and funded copies of the template that a two-transaction proof cannot exclude.
- **Proposition 10b.** Its statement that a calibrated reward on events the agent can move rewards manipulation is clear, correct and important. The authors are right to restrict the training claim because of it.
- **The lease.** Its clock-free check, comparing two heights in the same chain, is clean and well tested.
- **Traceability.** Every number is traced to a public ledger and code.

## Major concerns

### 1. What the integration adds is not shown, and the alternatives it must beat are not considered

The paper states that every part has a precedent, so it stands or falls on a property that only the combination provides. The one offered, a record "complete against an operator's many identities" (introduction, section 11), is not delivered. Completeness holds per identity. Across an operator's identities nothing is complete: the operator can discard identities and present the luckiest, as section 10 itself says. The bond puts a price on each further identity, and Theorem 1 counts them. That is pricing, not completeness, and the contrast drawn with Foresight Arena should be "a per-identity bond versus rate limiting", not "closed versus open".

Section 7 compares Bitcoin with a registry run by a single lab, consortium or state, which is the weakest alternative. The two strongest are never discussed:

- append-only transparency logs with independent witnesses and monitors, the certificate and key transparency family, which give non-equivocation and completeness with no single operator;
- general-purpose public chains with contracts, where expiry, amounts and destinations can be enforced. That would remove most of section 8's workarounds, and it is where ERC-8004 and ERC-8370 already live.

The case for Bitcoin rests on three informal arguments, which the paper says are not theorems. One of them, that competing labs will not use a rival's registry, is asserted as fact. I would like to see a property-by-property comparison: Escapement, a witnessed transparency log plus a per-identity bond, and a contract chain.

### 2. Theorem 1's condition is stronger than presented and cannot be checked

The text calls H0 "a property of the baseline, not of the agent". That is not accurate.

- **What the proof needs.** The tower-property step takes the expectation of each ratio given what is known when the round opens. An identity's forecasts can use non-public information, so the step needs the baseline to be the law of each outcome given public information and the identity's own forecasts. That is the statement that the identity has no edge. With private information the ratio's expectation exceeds one (Proposition 7). The theorem's "whatever an identity's strategy" therefore needs the qualifier "using only public information".
- **Practical force.** H0 also requires the baseline to be exactly calibrated given everything public. That is an efficient-market assumption no relying party can verify.
- **Biased baselines.** Any public, systematic bias in the baseline lets an agent with no private skill raise its score, and the record will certify that as edge.
- **Copying.** "Everything public" includes forecasts other identities have disclosed. A baseline that does not absorb them lets a copier repeat a skilled agent's disclosed forecasts in its next entry. The red team raised this (RT-06), and the manuscript does not mention it.

**Requested.** A robustness version of Theorem 1. If the baseline is within a stated divergence of the public conditional law each round, bound the growth rate of a public-information strategy, and from it the time for one identity to fake a record. The abstract and introduction should state the condition and its strength.

**Time to build a record.** The worked example is the most favourable case possible: an agent that knows each probability, against a public forecast of one half. At realistic edge over a good baseline, the rounds needed grow in inverse proportion to the edge, and each round lasts until its slowest event resolves. Please give the wall-clock time a skilled agent needs at realistic edges and resolution horizons. If the answer is years, the separation result has little practical force, and the paper should say so.

### 3. Standing measures capability, not alignment, and covers few agents

The paper notes that the record "does not separate honesty". The consequence that matters most for an AI-safety readership is not drawn. Edge is a product of capability, which a misaligned agent has as much as an aligned one. Standing confers access (section 5), so the scheme routes trust fastest to the most capable agents, whatever their goals. This belongs in section 4, in the list of abuses in section 10, in section 12, and in the abstract.

Separately, promise history counts only against an agent, because kept promises can be wash-traded. For an agent that does not forecast on listed exogenous events, which describes most deployed agents (coding, purchasing, support), standing reduces to age, bond size and a count of breaches. That is bonded reputation, without the record's separation result. The scope should be stated plainly.

### 4. The training claim is overstated, internally inconsistent, and narrow

1. **Selection by the lab.** Section 5 says the lab cannot choose after the fact which reward it meant to use. The next sentence says the lab "can commit many reward definitions and use one". It can also train many models under many identities and report the best. Either require that every committed definition be reported (the lab's complete chain makes the count visible, a real benefit worth stating), or narrow the claim. The abstract's "neither agent nor lab can select after the fact" should be narrowed in any case.
2. **Threat model.** During RL training the lab's harness, not the agent, decides which claims count. The agent-side selection that the record prevents is a deployment threat, not a training one. What remains in training is selection by the lab, which an ordinary timestamped hash already prevents for a single definition. Please name the training threat that on-chain completeness addresses and a lab's own committed log does not.
3. **Scope.** Restricting the claim to events the agent cannot influence excludes the case that matters for agents that act in the world. The research question asks about shaping what agents are trained to do. The answer delivered is calibration on exogenous forecasts, and the abstract and conclusion should say so.
4. **Proposition 10 as stated in the text is wrong.** The relative log score of a kept claim is bounded above by minus the log of its baseline probability. The expected reward rises towards a finite bound, with no finite maximiser; it does not rise "without limit". The repository's appendix states this correctly.
5. **The reward is not well defined.** It adds a dimensionless log score to forfeited stakes in sats, with no exchange rate, and Proposition 9 covers only the forecast term. Section 7 then says training rewards are denominated in sats, while section 5 says sats are not proposed as a training objective.
6. **Evidence.** The only evidence is a one-parameter recalibration learner, which the text rightly calls textbook. There is no experiment with a model, and no account of feasibility: how many resolved claims a run needs, at what resolution latency and fee.

### 5. The adoption analysis does not support the positive claims made from it

Propositions 11 and 12 are standard (unraveling [@milgrom1981good; @grossman1981informational; @jovanovic1982truthful] and coordination with multiple equilibria [@farrell1985standardization]), and both of their governing parameters are unmeasured. That some parties "gain whether or not anyone else adopts" is assumed, not shown. In the model a reader's gain is proportional to the share of agents with records, and the cited news report shows that agent insurance is sold, not that insurers would price records.

The agent-based model is used unevenly. Its tipping point and seeding results are reported as support, but its negative result, harmful actors cycling through identities at a profit, is set aside because "the model is too simple". It cannot be too simple for one and not the other.

The cycling result also comes from a pricing rule. In model/sim.py, readers price a young record at the mean of all joiners, not of newcomers. Readers who discount newcomers, the cheap-pseudonym equilibrium [@friedman2001social], would cut the cycling profit, but they would put the cost on honest newcomers. That trade-off, between deterring re-entry and excluding the poor and the new, is the real question, and it meets the exclusion concern of section 10.

The sentence that a one-off identity cost large enough to stop cycling also stopped honest agents joining is not reproducible from the published simulation or the ledger. The adoption function has no such parameter, and the figure appears only in the red-team notes. That contradicts section 9's statement that every result enters through the ledger.

The text also drops two conditions the appendix states: that a·x stays below one, and the second condition for reaching high adoption with seed readers. It does not specify how seed readers combine with the others. If the reading share is the larger of the two, a seed share just above 2c leaves a low interior equilibrium rather than high adoption.

### 6. The enforcement layer undermines the record it is meant to protect, and some limits are missing

- **Guardians.** Section 8 finds that one guardian can end an identity for good and keep its value, and that the guardians together can write entries the agent never made. The record is then a record of what the agent or its guardians wrote. Yet the abstract, introduction and conclusion still describe a complete record of what the agent committed to, which "cannot be ... quietly trimmed". Either find a fix with today's script (for example, renewal that also needs the agent's key, or scorers that discount entries after a renew) or carry this limit into the abstract and conclusion.
- **Per-identity limits.** Rate limits, per-entry caps and tiers all apply per identity. An action split across identities, entries and relying parties passes every check. Tiers are never defined, and the red team found them self-labelled per leaf. A promise can be written as a claim to avoid a stake. The per-entry cap holds only if someone checks the whole sum tree. None of this is in the manuscript.
- **Veto finality.** On the paper's own figures, a Tier 2 veto is as final as a six-confirmation payment, and no more. "Holds against everyone short of a bribed large pool" overstates it.
- **Recourse.** The identity bond can be spent only by the principal after its timelock, yet sections 6 and 10 present it as recourse and as liability landing on the principal's coins. Stake custody for promises is never specified, so the deterrence condition of section 4 has no mechanism behind its stake.

### 7. Completeness of the manuscript, disclosure and reproducibility

- **Proofs.** The paper relies on "the formal appendix" for Propositions 1 to 8, 10b, 13 and 14, but the manuscript has no appendix; the proofs exist only in the repository. The text's own numbering starts at Proposition 9, and the verification table cites Proposition 13, which the text never states. The appendix must be part of the submission.
- **Red team.** The red-team report should be supplementary material. Several limits it forced are absent from the text, listed under concern 6 and in my notes.
- **Section 9.** It presents "six levels of verification" without disclosure. Who were the "helpers" who searched for prior art: people or AI systems, how were they instructed, and what did they not search (the text says they stated it, but does not report it)? A writing tool's review desks are presented as a verification level, with none of their findings reported. These are the authors' own checks, not independent evidence, and the abstract should not lead with them.
- **Sources.** Nearly every Bitcoin-technical source is marked unchecked, and no source has reading notes. The measured inputs (hashrate, fee rates, pool shares) need dates and archived API responses. The hardware efficiency, hardware price and data-centre power behind section 7's clock costs are not cited in the paper at all. The lab should be cited as a versioned archive.

## Minor concerns

- **Bitcoin's guarantees** are listed three different ways: four unfakeable things in the introduction, a different four in section 2, then "three hard guarantees and one soft one". Section 2's claim that anyone can verify "all of this from block headers and inclusion proofs" contradicts section 8, where light proofs cannot show the newest entry.
- **Terms for two audiences.** Charter, tier, d, season, and the many roles (principal, guardian, scorer, resolver, adjudicator, baseline provider, relying party) are undefined or scattered. AI readers need sats, vB, WU, EH/s, outpoint, Taproot tweak, TRUC and pay-to-anchor explained. Bitcoin readers need e-values, Ville's inequality, proper scoring rules and performative prediction. A table of terms and roles, and one figure showing the tick chain's script tree and the record's data flow, would help both.
- **Theorem blocks.** Set the theorem and propositions in labelled theorem blocks so they can be cross-referenced.
- **Undefined score.** The "worst outcome" for an undisclosed leaf is undefined for a likelihood-ratio score.
- **Unusable rule.** "λ must exceed the number of identities in use" cannot be applied without a way to count identities.
- **Uninformative simulations.** The simulation of 256 identities and the "dropping misses" simulation reproduce closed forms; report the exact values instead.
- **Garbled phrase** in section 4: "20 claims one-claim rounds".
- **Stray placement.** Section 8 places "![](tb_blocks)", which is not among the paper's tables.
- **Quotation marks.** The phrase attributed to BIP 345 in section 2 is in quotation marks but has no kept passage.
- **Citation placement.** The zeke2026 citation is attached to what is said to be new in the lease.
- **Overreach on instrumental convergence.** "Almost any goal" goes beyond Turner et al.'s result [@turner2019optimal]. The same argument also says agents value escaping guardians and leases, which should be stated.
- **Year-to-date figure.** "Across 2026" is a year-to-date figure; say so and date it.
- **Length.** The abstract, introduction and conclusion are over their word limits.

## Missing work (kinds, for the authors to search and add)

- Transparency logs with witness cosigning and monitors, as the non-blockchain route to completeness.
- The theory of sybil-proof reputation functions.
- Proof-of-personhood and attestation schemes as alternative costly identities.
- Contract platforms as substrates.
- Testing by betting under approximate or misspecified nulls, which bears directly on a robust Theorem 1.
- AI-safety work on evaluation awareness and sandbagging, which bears on "behaves well where it is recorded".

## Recommendation: major revision

In its present form I could not recommend acceptance. The abstract, introduction and conclusion claim more than the body supports:

- a resource "no participant can ... backdate or edit";
- a record "complete against an operator's many identities";
- a reward "neither agent nor lab can select after the fact";
- adoption that parties "who gain alone can push past";
- a record that "cannot be backdated, forked or quietly trimmed".

The body has internal contradictions (training rewards in sats, recourse from a bond nobody can seize, light verification). The proofs are not in the manuscript. And by the authors' own account, no mechanism or theorem is new.

I recommend major revision rather than rejection because the body is unusually honest, most problems can be fixed by restatement, and the integration may be worth a careful design paper. A revision should:

- state H0 as the joint condition it is and add a robustness result, with time-to-record at realistic edge;
- show a property the integration has that a witnessed transparency log plus a bond, or a contract chain, lacks;
- state the capability-not-alignment limit, the forecasting-only scope and the guardian problem in the abstract and conclusion;
- fix Proposition 10, the reward definition and the lab-selection contradiction;
- rebuild or reframe the adoption simulation with newcomer pricing and multi-period agents, and publish every run it cites;
- include the formal appendix and the red-team report, and disclose who did the searches and reviews.

If the revision cannot show what the integration adds over those alternatives, I would recommend rejection.
